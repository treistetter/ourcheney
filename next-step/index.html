A community partnership agreement

One Voice

A community partnership agreement is a written framework that defines how project sponsors and the neighborhood will negotiate, make decisions, protect public land, and recognize community approval.

It matters because community meetings and opportunities to provide feedback are not the same as community consent. Agreeing to the rules first makes participation transparent, accountable, and enforceable. The residents of Summerhill will speak with one loud voice and through unity secure the best future for the neighborhood.

Community Agreement Framework for the Cheney Stadium Project

Purpose

Atlanta Public Schools, Atlanta Track Club, and Organized Neighbors of Summerhill would agree to a formal process for deciding whether the neighborhood supports the proposed indoor track facility at Cheney Stadium.

The purpose is to make clear that community meetings and opportunities to provide feedback are not the same as community approval.

Community Ownership of the Land

The preferred structure would be for the land used by the project to be transferred to a new community-controlled nonprofit organization.

That organization would hold the land permanently for the benefit of Summerhill and would lease it to Atlanta Track Club, APS, or another approved operator for the specific facility agreed to by the neighborhood.

The community organization would not operate the track facility day to day. Its role would be to protect the land, the public benefits, and the long-term commitments made to the neighborhood.

The lease would allow the facility to be built and operated while ensuring that the land could not later be sold, expanded, reassigned, or converted to another use without community involvement.

If transferring ownership is shown to be legally impossible, the parties would negotiate another structure that gives the community similarly strong and permanent rights over the property.

Permanent Community Funding

The final agreement would include a substantial and permanent financial contribution for the benefit of Summerhill.

The exact amount would be negotiated later. It would take into account the value of the public land, the length and value of the lease, the public investment in the project, and the impacts of placing the facility in the neighborhood.

The contribution could be placed in an endowment or another permanently restricted fund. It would not be replaced by short-term grants, sponsorships, discounts, occasional programming, or voluntary annual donations.

The fund would support parks, recreation, youth opportunities, public spaces, and other neighborhood priorities.

Local Governance

Summerhill residents would have meaningful control over both the community land organization and the community fund.

The governing boards would include a majority or substantial representation selected through a transparent neighborhood process. APS and Atlanta Track Club could participate, but they would not control the organizations or have the power to redirect the land or money for their own purposes.

The final agreement would explain how community representatives are selected, how decisions are made, how finances are reported, how conflicts of interest are handled, and which decisions require a broader neighborhood vote.

Public Access and Facility Operations

The final agreement would include clear commitments concerning how the facility will serve APS students, Summerhill residents, and the public.

The agreement would address public access to the track and related facilities, neighborhood and youth programming, operating hours, event frequency, parking and traffic, lighting and noise, security and maintenance, construction impacts, and the process for handling complaints and violations.

These commitments would be written into enforceable agreements rather than left as general statements of intent.

Project Disclosure

Before asking ONS or the neighborhood to support the project, APS and Atlanta Track Club would provide a complete description of what they intend to build and how they intend to obtain approval.

That disclosure would identify the project site and the amount of land involved, the proposed building size and height, site plans and renderings, parking and transportation plans, expected uses and operating hours, the proposed lease or ownership structure, public-access plans, and all zoning permits, variances, rezonings, or other approvals being requested.

If the project sponsors believe the facility does not require a special-use permit, they would provide the neighborhood with a written explanation from the City stating how the project will be classified.

Negotiation Process

ONS would appoint a neighborhood negotiating group to work with APS and Atlanta Track Club.

The group would negotiate the major parts of the final agreement, including ownership or control of the land, the amount and structure of the community fund, public access, neighborhood oversight, project design and operations, enforcement, and what happens if the facility closes, expands, or changes operators.

The negotiators could recommend an agreement, but they could not approve the project on behalf of the neighborhood.

Neighborhood Ratification

Once negotiations are complete, the proposed agreement and final project plans would be released publicly before a neighborhood vote.

ONS would hold a public meeting to explain the proposal, answer questions, and hear comments. Eligible ONS voters would then decide whether to approve the agreement and support the project.

A vote in favor would mean:

ONS supports the specific project presented to the neighborhood, provided that the agreed community ownership, funding, access, and operating protections are formally adopted and carried out.

A vote against the agreement would mean that ONS has not approved the project.

Relationship to Permitting

If the neighborhood approves the final agreement, ONS would present that decision to NPU-V and recommend support for the specific zoning applications reviewed by the neighborhood.

ONS support would be limited to the project, plans, approvals, and conditions included in the final agreement. It would not automatically apply to later changes, expansions, additional variances, or a different project.

Material Changes

Major changes would have to return to ONS for review.

This would include a larger or taller building, the use of additional land, reduced public access, more events or more intensive commercial use, a new operator, an extended lease, weaker community funding, or a different use of the property.

Depending on the importance of the change, ONS could require additional negotiations or another neighborhood vote.

No Claim of Community Support Before Approval

Until the final agreement is approved through the neighborhood process, APS and Atlanta Track Club would not claim that ONS, Summerhill, or the neighborhood supports the project.

They could accurately say that they held meetings, received comments, or were negotiating with ONS. They could not treat attendance, survey responses, informal conversations, or a lack of organized opposition as community approval.

Intended Outcome

The framework is intended to produce a final agreement that gives the community permanent ownership or enforceable control of the land, provides Summerhill with a substantial and permanent financial asset, gives residents meaningful authority over those community assets, establishes enforceable public-access and neighborhood protections, requires disclosure of the full project before approval, establishes neighborhood support through a formal vote, and prevents later project changes from relying on the original approval without further review.

Signing this framework would not mean that ONS supports the project.

It would mean that APS, Atlanta Track Club, and ONS agree on what must happen before neighborhood support can legitimately be claimed.
